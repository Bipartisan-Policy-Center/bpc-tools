Methodology & Data Sources

How This Is Calculated

The calculator estimates how many taxpayers would need to give the maximum creditable amount to fund scholarships for a given number of students (student side), or how many students a given share of taxpayers could fund (donor side).

Students. The default population is all children ages 5–17; public school enrollment (2024–25, excluding full-virtual schools) is an alternative. Income eligibility is approximated by the share of children ages 6–17 in households below 300% of the federal poverty line, a conservative proxy for the program’s 300% of area median income limit. Alternatives are Treasury’s 96% national estimate and public school students at schools located in 2027 qualified census tracts, which qualify for tutoring and special needs scholarships under the proposed high-poverty safe harbor.

Qualified census tracts. The QCT option counts students enrolled at public schools located in a 2027 qualified census tract (QCT), based on a BPC spatial join of NCES EDGE 2024–25 school locations and enrollment to Census TIGER/Line 2020 census tracts (2023 tracts for Connecticut) and HUD’s 2027 QCT list. It includes schools reporting enrollment above zero and excludes full-virtual schools. Every student at a school in a QCT is treated as eligible without an income test. Private schools and schools that would qualify under the proposed 80% residency test are not counted, so the estimate understates the students the safe harbor could reach.

Donors. Potential donors are taxpayers with enough federal tax liability to use a $1,700 credit, estimated from IRS returns by income: all returns with adjusted gross income of $50,000 or more, half at $25,000–$50,000, and 10% at $10,000–$25,000. A second spouse is added for joint returns likely to owe enough for a $3,400 credit. These weights are BPC assumptions.

Funding. Each donor gives $1,700 and SGOs keep 10%: students served = donors × $1,700 × 0.90 ÷ scholarship cost. The funding box compares total donations with each state’s public K–12 revenue from federal, state, and local sources. Results assume donations are spread evenly and do not account for state tax credits or other uses of SGO funds.

Income eligibility · Default measure

300% of Area Median Income (Proxy)

The FSTC uses 300% of HUD Area Median Income, which varies by metropolitan area and county; there is no single statewide number. This calculator uses the Census ACS share of children ages 6–17 in households below 300% of the federal poverty line (2020–2024 five-year estimates, ACS Table B17024) as a conservative proxy. Because the poverty line is lower than AMI in most areas, this proxy understates the true AMI-eligible population.

Eligibility · High-poverty safe harbor

Schools in Qualified Census Tracts

Under Treasury’s proposed regulations, an SGO that funds individual academic tutoring or special needs services for students selected by their school based on need may treat those students as income-eligible without verifying household income if the school is located in a HUD qualified census tract or certifies that at least 80% of its students live in one. The SGO must obtain an annual third-party audit (REG-117199-25, proposed § 1.25F-3(c)(6)(iii)(C)). The safe harbor does not apply to tuition.

This option counts public school students enrolled in schools located in a 2027 qualified census tract, based on a BPC match of NCES 2024–25 school locations and enrollment to census tracts. It excludes full-virtual schools and does not count private schools or schools that would qualify under the 80% residency test, so it understates the students the safe harbor could reach. Most students in these schools would also meet the income limit; Treasury estimates the safe harbor makes about 170,000 additional children eligible nationally.

Income eligibility · Alternative measure

Treasury’s National Estimate (96%)

Treasury estimates that 96% of children ages 5–17 in states that had opted in as of July 2026 would be eligible under the proposed regulations (REG-117199-25, economic analysis, Table 2). Treasury modeled the actual 300% of area median income limit using 2024 ACS microdata and HUD income limits but did not publish state-level estimates. This calculator applies the 96% to every state; actual shares will be lower in high-income states and higher in low-income states.

FSTC · §25F · Treasury Proposed Rule

$1,700 Credit Limit: Per Person, Not Per Return

How Treasury’s proposed regulations treat married couples filing jointly.

📄
What the proposed rule says Treasury and the IRS “interpret section 25F to limit the credit amount that may be claimed to $1,700 per individual taxpayer, allowing married couples filing jointly to potentially claim up to $3,400 on their joint tax return provided that each spouse has made qualified contributions of up to $1,700.” The combined credit is limited by the couple’s tax liability. (REG-117199-25, proposed § 1.25F-2(b)(2), Federal Register, October 2, 2026.)
⚖️
Status This provision is in the proposed rule, not the binding temporary rule. Taxpayers may rely on the proposed rule for contributions made on or after January 1, 2027, until final regulations are issued. Comments are due December 1, 2026.
